Losing or gaining weight

Body recomposition: losing fat and building muscle at the same time

You often hear that you have to choose: cut or bulk. The research is more nuanced. Losing fat while gaining muscle is possible, especially for some profiles, as long as you accept that the scale tells you almost nothing about what is happening.

In short

Recomposition works mainly for beginners, people returning after a break and those with fat to lose. It takes regular strength training, high protein (around 1.6 to 2.2 g per kilo) and calories close to maintenance or in a slight deficit. Weight changes little: you also need to track your lifts and your waist.

Body recomposition: the definition

Recomposition means changing the body’s composition, less fat mass and more muscle mass, without necessarily changing weight. On the scale, a kilo of fat lost and a kilo of muscle gained cancel out; in the mirror and on the bar, they do not.

That is the difference from a cut, which aims first at losing fat while limiting muscle loss, and from a bulk, which accepts a little fat in order to build faster.

Body recomposition: who it works for

A review on the subject concludes that recomposition is well documented in people new to strength training, those returning after a break, and those with higher body fat; it is also possible in trained lifters, but slower and less consistent [1].

The closer you are to your muscular potential and the leaner you are, the harder it becomes to do both at once. At that stage, alternating bulking and cutting phases is often still more effective.

Calories for recomposition: maintenance or a slight deficit

There is no single number. Most approaches aim for intake close to maintenance, or a modest deficit in people with fat to lose. A large deficit can still allow muscle gain in some profiles: in a four-week trial, young men in a steep deficit, with intense training and protein at 2.4 g per kilo, gained about 1.2 kg of lean mass while losing 4.8 kg of fat, compared with 0.1 kg at 1.2 g per kilo [2].

That trial is short and involves a specific profile: it shows that it is possible, not that it is the rule.

Protein for recomposition: how much per day

Protein is the lever that comes up in every study. A meta-analysis puts the benefit on lean mass at up to about 1.6 g per kilo per day on average, with an upper margin around 2.2 g [3]. In a deficit, aiming for the top of the range is a reasonable precaution.

Basal applies 2.2 g per kilo of reference weight when the goal is to lose weight with at least three sessions a week, and 1.8 g for maintenance with the same training. The details are in how much protein per day.

Measuring recomposition: beyond the scale

Since weight changes little, it is not enough on its own. Three simple indicators, together, say much more: the weight trend over several weeks (daily weight fluctuations), your waist measurement taken at the same spot each week, and how your loads change on the same exercises (estimated 1RM). Photos in the same light are a good complement.

Bioimpedance scales give a body fat percentage, but it varies with hydration from one day to the next: follow its trend rather than its value.

Recomposition in Basal: weight and loads side by side

In the Progress tab, the “Weight and loads” card brings together two recorded facts: what your weight did over the period, and on how many of the movements you track your loads have progressed. If your loads go up while your weight goes down, that is the most accessible sign that the loss is not coming at the expense of muscle.

The card needs a goal of losing or gaining weight, at least four weigh-ins over at least 14 days, and at least two movements repeated over the period. It calculates no body fat percentage: it states the facts and leaves you to draw the conclusion. It is free.

What we don’t know

Most recomposition studies are short, from a few weeks to a few months, and measure lean mass, which includes water and glycogen, not just muscle. We cannot predict whether a given person will recompose, or how fast. The figures above are group averages.
