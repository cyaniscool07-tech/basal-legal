Choosing an app

An alternative to MyFitnessPal, made in France

This page is written by the developer of Basal. So I am both judge and party, and the most useful comparison I can offer is not to speak ill of an app that millions of people use very well — it is to say precisely what mine does differently, and what it does not do at all.

Let’s start with what Basal will not do as well

A food database is built over time and with its users. Apps installed for fifteen years have a head start that nothing catches up in a few months, and for an obscure processed product, they will find an entry where Basal will not.

  • Basal exists in English, French, Spanish and German, no further. And its food database is designed for France: if you live elsewhere, some of the products in your supermarket will not be in it.
  • No iPad layout: on a tablet, the app runs in iPhone compatibility mode.
  • Two food databases, each with its limit. Everyday foods come from ANSES’s Ciqual table (over 3,000 foods, French reference values); packaged products come from Open Food Facts, an open database fed by volunteers, whose quality varies from one product to another.
  • No news feed, no messaging, no conversational coach. Basal’s Community is only for sharing recipes, routines and programmes. If you are looking for a social network, Basal will disappoint you.
  • No import from another app. You start from a blank page: your history stays where it is.

If one of these points is a deal-breaker, the rest will not change anything, and you might as well know now.

What Basal does differently

Tracking is free, complete and has no time limit

Calories, macronutrients, barcode scanner, weight tracking, activity, steps, unlimited strength training, personal recipes, exporting your data: all of it is free. There is no trial that expires, no daily food quota, and no scanner reserved for subscribers.

The Premium subscription — €4.99 a month or €39.99 a year in the eurozone, shown in your currency in the store — adds the analyses (the weekly review and its history, metabolism over time, volume per muscle group, protein on training days compared with rest days, advanced nutrition strategies) and three meal assistants that organise your meals, prepare your week and optimise it. Recipes, meal planning and the shopping list stay free. If you never pay, you keep a whole tracking app.

No ads, no selling of data

There is no banner, no interstitial, no video to watch, and your data is sold to no one. This is not a sales promise, it is a consequence of the model: an app that lives on a subscription needs neither your attention nor your profile.

No mandatory account

You can install Basal and use it fully without ever creating an account. In that case, your profile, your weight and your meals stay on your phone and are sent nowhere. The account exists, it is optional, and it is for getting your data back on another device — the data then goes to a server located in Ireland, in the European Union, and deleting the account erases it.

Nutrition and strength training talk to each other

This is the difference that motivated the app. A strength-training log ignores what you eat; a nutrition app ignores what you lift. Basal knows both, so it can answer questions neither category handles alone: how many sets each muscle group really gets over the week, what you eat on rest days compared with training days, and whether your lifts go up while your weight goes down.

The details of these three analyses are on the nutrition and strength training page.

A metabolism that learns, not a fixed formula

Many apps calculate your expenditure once at sign-up, with an equation and an activity factor picked from a list, then never come back to it. Basal starts from the same equation, then compares what you log with how your weight actually changes to refine your expenditure over the weeks. It is explained on the adaptive metabolism page, and you can do the starting calculation yourself with the calculator.

And the tone

It is hard to put in a comparison table, yet it is what comes up most often among people who leave a tracking app.

Basal observes, it does not prescribe. A day above your target is never presented as a failure, and the current day is never counted as missed before it is over. A missed day triggers no guilt-inducing reminder. The weekly review states how reliable your data is before stating any numbers: with three days logged out of seven, it tells you so, instead of passing noise off as fact.

The app is for adults only. Calorie tracking in teenagers is a documented risk factor for eating disorders, and that is why the rating is set at 18+ in both stores.

In short

Choose Basal if

you want free, unlimited tracking, in English, French, Spanish or German, that links your eating and your training sessions, with no ads and no forced account.

Stay where you are if

you need another language, a very large food database (especially outside France), a social network with a news feed, or to bring over an existing history.

Since tracking is free and needs no account, trying it requires no sign-up. If it does not suit you, uninstall it: nothing has gone anywhere else.
