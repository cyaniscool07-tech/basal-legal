Calories and expenditure

Why your weight changes from one day to the next

The scale shows a kilo more than yesterday. It is an accurate measurement, and yet it says almost nothing about what you care about. A scale weighs a total: bones, muscle, fat, water, blood, and everything still passing through your system. The total moves constantly, and its most volatile parts have nothing to do with fat.

In short

One extra kilo overnight is almost never fat: you would have to eat about 7,700 kcal too many to gain one. The gap comes from water (glycogen, salt, training, cycle), from what is still being digested and from weighing conditions. Weight therefore varies by 1 to 2 kg every day: weigh yourself under the same conditions and read the seven-day average.

Let’s start with the arithmetic

One kilogram of fat tissue represents around 7,700 kcal. To really gain a kilo of fat in one day, you would therefore have to eat about 7,700 kcal above your expenditure — for most people, nearly three times their daily needs, in a single day.

It is not impossible, but it is rare, and above all: it is not what happened if the scale moved by a kilo overnight. The number comes from somewhere else.

Where the gap really comes from

  • Glycogen and its water. Your muscles and liver store glycogen, and each gram stored holds about three grams of water. Between full and depleted stores, the difference is counted in kilos — without a gram of fat having moved.
  • Sodium. A salty meal increases water retention for one to two days. The effect is mechanical and temporary.
  • Gut content. Part of what you ate and drank is still in your system. It is real weight, but it is not you.
  • Recent training. A hard session causes local inflammation and water retention in the muscles worked, which shows on the scale for a day or two.
  • The menstrual cycle, which produces regular, perfectly normal fluid shifts over several days.
  • Weighing conditions. Before or after the toilet, dressed or not, morning or evening: the gap between two readings on the same day commonly reaches one to two kilos.

A single weigh-in mostly measures noise. It is not a fault of your scale or your discipline: it is the nature of the signal. Body weight varies by one to two kilos around its real value, every day, for everyone.

What to look at instead

The solution is not to weigh yourself less often — it is the opposite. The more data points you have, the more reliable the average becomes. What needs to change is the number you look at.

A seven-day rolling average absorbs shifts in water, salt and digestion, because they cancel out over a week, whereas a change in fat mass does not. The average curve goes up or down when something real happens, and stays flat when nothing does.

Weigh yourself every day and only read the average: it is the routine that asks the most regularity and the least interpretation.

A few reading tips

  • One week is not enough to conclude. It usually takes two to three weeks of averages to tell a trend from a fluctuation.
  • A plateau is not a stop. Fluid shifts can mask several weeks of real change, then clear all at once — the scale then “catches up” abruptly.
  • Weigh yourself under the same conditions: on waking, after the toilet, before eating or drinking. What matters is not the condition you choose, it is that it is always the same.
