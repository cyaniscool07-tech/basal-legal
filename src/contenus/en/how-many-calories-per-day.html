Calories and expenditure

How many calories a day?

The 2,000 kcal figure printed on food labels is not your need. It is a reference value for comparing products with each other. Your real need depends on your height, weight, age, sex and above all on what you do with your days.

Ballpark figures by profile

Here is the estimated daily expenditure of four profiles, from the least to the most active. It is the number of calories that keeps weight stable: no loss, no gain.

Woman, 30, 1.65 m, 62 kg1,610 to 2,310 kcal
Woman, 60, 1.62 m, 68 kg1,480 to 2,120 kcal
Man, 30, 1.78 m, 78 kg2,100 to 3,010 kcal
Man, 60, 1.75 m, 82 kg1,940 to 2,790 kcal

The range goes from “sedentary” (under 5,000 steps a day, no sport) to “active” (6 to 7 sessions a week or over 10,000 steps a day). For the same profile, the gap is often more than 700 kcal: activity weighs more than age.

How to work out your own need

The calculation has two steps. First the basal metabolic rate, the energy your body spends at complete rest to breathe, keep its temperature and run its organs. The most widely used formula today is Mifflin-St Jeor.

Then this basal metabolic rate is multiplied by an activity factor, from 1.2 for a sedentary life to 1.9 for a physical job with training. The result is your daily energy expenditure, often called TDEE.

To lose weight: how many calories less?

To lose weight, you need to eat a little less than you spend. A gap of 10 to 20% below your daily expenditure is a reasonable starting point for most people: enough to see a result within a few weeks, not so much that energy or muscle suffer.

For the moderately active 30-year-old woman above (about 2,080 kcal), that gives an intake of 1,660 to 1,870 kcal a day. All the details, including the pace of loss to expect, are in the calorie deficit guide.

Going very low does not speed things up much. It is generally considered that an intake below 1,200 kcal for a woman or 1,500 kcal for a man should not be kept up without medical supervision. Hunger, fatigue and muscle loss quickly take back what you thought you had gained.

To build muscle: how many calories more?

Conversely, building muscle calls for a slight surplus: around 5 to 10% above expenditure, with enough protein and training that progresses. Beyond that, the surplus mostly goes to fat. The bulking guide gives the numbers.

What the formula cannot know

A formula describes a population, not a person. For a given individual, the typical error is around 10%, or 200 kcal a day either way. That is often exactly the gap between “I am losing” and “I am stalling”.

The only way to know your real need is to measure it: log what you eat and weigh yourself regularly for two to three weeks, then compare. If your weight (as a trend, not day to day) does not move, your average intake is your true maintenance. That is the principle of adaptive metabolism.

In practice

  • Work out your expenditure with a formula to get a starting point, not a truth.
  • To lose weight, take off 10 to 20%. To build muscle, add 5 to 10%.
  • Keep that number for two to three weeks before judging it: one day’s weight says nothing.
  • Then adjust in small steps, 100 to 150 kcal, based on your weight trend.
