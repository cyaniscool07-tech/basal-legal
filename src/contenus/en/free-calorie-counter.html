Choosing an app

A free calorie app, with no subscription

Every nutrition tracking app is free to download. So the word no longer says much. What does say something is the list of what you can do without paying — and that list has shrunk almost everywhere in recent years.

The four ways of saying “free”

When an app presents itself as free, it usually covers one of these situations. They are not at all equal.

  • Free with ads. You do not pay in money. The model assumes your attention is worth something, so the app tries to bring you back often, which is not always in your interest.
  • Free with a quota. A number of foods per day, a number of recipes, a history limited to a few days. The app works until the exact moment it becomes useful to you.
  • Free during the trial. Seven days, sometimes three. After that, it is a paid app that asked you to put your data in first.
  • Free, except the core feature. The most effective of the four: everything stays open except the thing that made you install the app. The barcode scanner has become the textbook case.

The scanner is the real test. Logging a meal by hand takes one to two minutes; the scanner takes three seconds. It decides whether you will keep going for a month or for three days. An app that puts it behind a subscription is not a free app with a paid option: it is a paid app with a demo.

What Basal keeps free

No subscription, no trial that expires, no quota, and no ads:

  • calories and macronutrients — protein, carbohydrates, fat, fibre — split by meal;
  • food search and the barcode scanner;
  • weight tracking, with trends and the moving average;
  • adaptive metabolism, which refines your expenditure from your real data;
  • strength training, unlimited: over 870 exercises, sets, loads, reps, supersets, rest timer, records and routines;
  • cardio sessions and steps, with the calories burned calculated for you;
  • your own recipes, with their nutrition calculated per serving;
  • a full export of your data, as JSON or CSV, at any time.

So what is paid

The analyses — the weekly review and its history, metabolism over time, volume per muscle group, protein on training days compared with rest days, advanced nutrition strategies — and three meal assistants that organise your meals, prepare your week and optimise it (each can be tried once for free). Recipes, meal planning and the shopping list stay free. €4.99 a month or €39.99 a year in the eurozone; the store shows the price in your currency.

The split follows a simple rule: logging and cooking are free; understanding and automating are paid. If you never pay, you keep a complete log that does not degrade over time. Nothing locks after thirty days.

Why this is not generosity

To be direct: this model exists because it is sustainable, not out of philanthropy. An app without ads does not need to capture your attention, and an app that does not sell data does not need your profile. That leaves the subscription, which is only justified if what it unlocks has value in itself — hence the choice never to hold basic tracking to ransom.

The downside is real and it is mine: many people will use Basal for years without ever paying. That is the price of a model in which you do not have to be held hostage.

And without an account

You can use Basal fully without creating an account. In that case, your profile, your weight and your meals stay on your phone and are sent nowhere. The details, including what works offline, are in a calorie app without an account. The account is optional and only serves to get your data back on another device; deleting it erases them from the server. The app is for adults only.
