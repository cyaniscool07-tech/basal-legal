Are you eating enough for what you lift?

The question sounds simple. Answering it takes two logs that almost never talk to each other: the gym one and the food one.

Two logs, no conversation

A nutrition app knows what you eat and ignores what you lift. A strength-training app knows what you lift and ignores what you eat. Each answers half of the question perfectly, and the missing half is exactly where the interesting answers are.

Three observations need both logs at once. None is spectacular. All can be checked on your own numbers.

1. How many sets each muscle really gets

Ask someone how many weekly sets their shoulders get and they will give a number. Count them in their log and the gap is often a factor of two. Sessions look alike, exercises repeat, and some muscle groups quietly drop out of the programme without anyone noticing.

This count is not trivial. A bench press works the chest, but also the triceps and front delts. Counting the set once for each muscle involved inflates the total artificially; counting it only for the chest erases the rest. The common practice is to count one set for the main muscle and half a set for each secondary muscle. It is a convention, not a truth — but it has the merit of being consistent from week to week, which is exactly what you ask of a benchmark.

Per-muscle totals do not add up to the number of sets in the session. It is confusing the first time: a 20-set session can produce 34 “sets per muscle” spread over six groups. The two numbers measure different things, and only the second says what each muscle received.

2. Rest days, the blind spot

The most common mistake is not eating too little on training days. It is eating markedly less on rest days, without having decided to. The day is organised differently, hunger drops, the post-workout snack disappears — and the protein with it.

Yet muscle protein synthesis stays elevated well after the session. Rest days are not days without needs; they are the days when building happens. Comparing your intake between the two categories is instructive precisely because you do not expect a gap.

Meta-analyses commonly put useful intakes for strength training around 1.6 to 2.2 g of protein per kilogram of body weight per day, with returns flattening beyond. It is not a magic threshold, and the range is wide because the studies are too. It serves to place an order of magnitude, not to judge a single day.

3. The one accessible sign you are not losing muscle

During weight loss, the real question is not how many kilos have gone, but what they were made of. A scale will never tell you: it weighs a total, not a composition.

There is one clue anyone can read: if your weight goes down while your loads go up, the loss is probably not coming out of muscle. The reverse deserves attention — weight dropping while loads collapse, over several weeks, is the clearest signal that a deficit is too aggressive or that protein is not keeping up. To compare sessions with different reps, the estimated 1RM puts your loads on the same scale.

Beware of enthusiasm. In a beginner, loads go up for months for purely neural reasons, without a gram of extra muscle. The signal is useful in someone who has been training for a while and whose progress has already slowed; in a beginner losing weight, it does not prove much.

What cross-checking cannot tell you

It measures no body composition. It does not replace bioimpedance, skinfolds or DEXA — and those three have their own margins of error anyway.

It depends entirely on what you log. Half-logged sessions make volume that really happened disappear; forgotten meals make intake look lower than it is. The conclusion will be wrong in the direction that hurts you.

It needs weeks. One week of volume says nothing about a neglected muscle: you need at least two weeks of data for a gap to become a trend rather than a scheduling accident. Same for protein, where a few days are enough to shift an average.

In practice

  • Count your sets per muscle over the week, not per session: that is the unit a programme is steered by.
  • Look at your rest-day protein separately from your training-day protein. That is where the gap hides.
  • Only call a muscle “neglected” after two weeks of data, never on one.
  • If your weight and your loads both go down over three weeks, raise your calories before raising your motivation.
  • Log the weeks that go wrong. They are what explain the month’s numbers.
