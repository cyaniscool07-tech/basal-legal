Losing or gaining weight

Calorie deficit: how to calculate it, what pace, mistakes to avoid

Losing weight always comes down to the same thing: spending a little more energy than you eat, for long enough. The whole difficulty lies in the “a little” and the “long enough”.

What a calorie deficit is

A calorie deficit is the gap between your daily energy expenditure and what you eat. If you spend 2,100 kcal and eat 1,750, your deficit is 350 kcal a day. The body covers the shortfall by drawing on its reserves, mainly fat if the deficit is moderate and protein is sufficient.

How to calculate your deficit

First you need your daily expenditure (often called TDEE). You can estimate it with the calculator or from the examples by profile. Then take 10 to 20% off that figure. Up to 25% can be held for a short period if the starting weight is high.

Woman, 30, 62 kg, moderately active (expenditure ≈ 2,080 kcal)1,660 to 1,870 kcal
Man, 30, 78 kg, moderately active (expenditure ≈ 2,710 kcal)2,170 to 2,440 kcal
Woman, 60, 68 kg, lightly active (expenditure ≈ 1,690 kcal)1,350 to 1,520 kcal

A percentage is better than a fixed number. The often-repeated “minus 500 kcal” is 18% of an active man’s expenditure, but nearly 30% of a small sedentary woman’s: the same advice becomes much harder to stick to.

What weight loss to expect

It is commonly estimated that about 7,700 kcal correspond to one kilo of fat. A deficit of 500 kcal a day therefore gives, in theory, a little under half a kilo a week.

A more personal benchmark: aim to lose 0.5 to 1% of your weight a week. At 62 kg that is 0.3 to 0.6 kg; at 90 kg, 0.45 to 0.9 kg. Beyond that, muscle loss and fatigue increase markedly.

The scale does not follow the theory day by day. Water, salt, carbs stored in muscle and, for women, the menstrual cycle move weight by one or two kilos with nothing to do with fat. Judge a deficit on the two-to-three-week trend, never on a single weigh-in. More in why your weight changes from one day to the next.

Signs of a deficit that is too aggressive

  • Constant hunger that ends in regular binges.
  • Fatigue setting in, sleep getting worse.
  • Training loads dropping, week after week.
  • Fast loss at first, then nothing despite the effort.

In those cases, raising calories a little is often more effective than tightening further.

Keeping muscle during a deficit

Two levers protect muscle mass: a high protein intake, commonly between 1.6 and 2.2 g per kilo of body weight, and keeping up strength training (details in how much protein per day). If your weight drops while your loads hold or go up, the loss is probably coming from fat. The topic is developed in nutrition for strength training.

Why a deficit ends up stalling

The more weight you lose, the less you spend: a lighter body costs less energy to carry. The body also trims its spontaneous activity a little (you move less without noticing). The deficit calculated at the start therefore shrinks on its own, and the loss slows down.

The answer is not to keep cutting, but to recalculate your expenditure from what is really happening, then adjust in small steps of 100 to 150 kcal. That is exactly what adaptive metabolism describes.

In practice

  • Work out your expenditure, then take off 10 to 20%.
  • Aim for 0.5 to 1% of your weight a week, measured on the trend.
  • Keep your protein high and your strength training going.
  • If the trend stops moving for three weeks, recalculate before cutting more.
  • Do not go below 1,200 kcal (women) or 1,500 kcal (men) without medical supervision.
