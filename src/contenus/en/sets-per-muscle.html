How many sets per muscle per week

Ask someone how many weekly sets their shoulders get, and they will give a number without hesitating. Count them in their log, and the gap is often a factor of two. It is not a memory problem: the unit we think in — the session — is not the one the muscle works in.

In short

Aim for roughly 10 to 20 sets per muscle group per week: that is the range the hypertrophy literature most often points to, with gains flattening at the top. Count a full set for the main muscle and half a set for each secondary muscle. If you are progressing with less, nothing forces you to do more.

Why a session total says nothing

“I did twenty sets today” is information about your fatigue, not about your programme. It says neither which muscles worked, nor in what proportion, nor how it adds up with the session the day before yesterday.

The muscle, for its part, does not know about sessions. It receives volume, recovers, receives more. The window that makes sense for it is the week, and the unit is the muscle group, not the movement.

How to count a set

This is the point that stumps everyone, because an exercise almost never works a single muscle. A bench press works the chest, but also the triceps and front delts. If you count the set once for every muscle involved, you inflate your totals artificially. If you count it only for the chest, you erase the rest.

The most common convention: a full set for the main muscle, half a set for each secondary muscle. It is not a physiological truth, it is a judgement call. Its merit is being consistent from week to week, which is exactly what you ask of a benchmark: it does not need to be exact, it needs to be stable.

A confusing consequence the first time: per-muscle totals do not add up to the number of sets in the session. A 20-set session can produce 34 “sets per muscle” spread over six groups. The two numbers measure different things, and only the second says what each muscle received.

What order of magnitude

The hypertrophy literature commonly puts the useful range at around ten to twenty weekly sets per muscle group, with returns flattening at the top of the range and large individual variability.

The range is wide because the studies are: populations, training levels and even the definition of an “effective” set vary from one protocol to the next. It serves to place an order of magnitude, not to judge a week. Someone on eight sets who is progressing has no reason to change anything.

What the number reveals best is not the total, it is the distribution. Imbalances always sit in the same place: lots of chest and biceps, little back, and legs that quietly drop out of the programme when the week gets busy.

Why we always get it wrong the same way

  • Sessions look alike. You think you are varying, the log says you have repeated the same four movements for three months.
  • Secondary muscles are invisible. Nobody counts the triceps in a press or the biceps in a row, although they often make up half the real volume of those groups.
  • You remember the hard sessions, not the missed ones. A week where leg day was skipped looks like a normal week in memory.
  • Warm-ups get mixed in with working sets depending on your mood, and the total becomes impossible to compare from week to week.

Doing it by hand, or not

Nothing stops you from counting yourself: go back over your last seven days, give each set to its main muscle and half a set to each secondary, add up by group. For one week it takes a quarter of an hour. The problem is not the first calculation, it is redoing it every week — that is where everyone gives up.
