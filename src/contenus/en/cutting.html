Cutting: how to lose fat without losing muscle

A cut is weight loss with one extra constraint: keeping the muscle you spent months building. What makes it work comes down to three settings, the rate of loss, protein and training, and one habit: watching the trend rather than the day’s weigh-in.

In short

Aim to lose 0.5 to 1% of your body weight a week, eat at least 1.6 g of protein per kilo (often more during a cut), and keep lifting heavy. The faster the loss, the larger the share of muscle lost tends to be.

Cutting: what it really is

Cutting means losing body fat while keeping as much muscle as possible. In energy terms nothing sets it apart from a diet: you need a calorie deficit. What changes is the priorities. Someone who simply wants to lose weight may accept losing a little muscle; someone coming out of a bulk wants to avoid it, and that is where the rate, protein and training matter.

Calorie deficit for a cut: how much to cut

A deficit is better thought of as a percentage of your expenditure than as a fixed number of calories: 500 kcal less is moderate for someone who burns 3,000 kcal and severe for someone who burns 1,800. Basal offers three settings, −10%, −15% and −20% of expenditure, and never shows a goal below your basal metabolic rate or below 800 kcal a day.

The starting point matters: if your expenditure is badly estimated, so is your deficit. That is why expenditure is then corrected using what you really eat and how your weight changes (work out your real expenditure).

Weight loss when cutting: what rate to aim for

Recommendations for natural bodybuilders put the useful rate at 0.5 to 1% of body weight a week [1]. At 80 kg, that is 0.4 to 0.8 kg a week.

Going faster costs muscle. In athletes, losing about 0.7% a week preserved lean mass and strength better than losing 1.4% a week, for the same amount of weight lost [2]. A slow cut lasts longer, but what it removes is mostly fat.

Read this rate on the trend, not on the day’s weigh-in: water and gut content move the scale by 1 to 2 kg from one day to the next (daily weight fluctuations).

Protein when cutting: how much per day

A meta-analysis of strength training puts the benefit of protein on lean mass at up to about 1.6 g per kilo per day on average, with an upper margin around 2.2 g [3]. In a deficit, and in people who are already lean, recommendations go higher, to 2.3 to 3.1 g per kilo of lean mass [1].

Basal applies 2.2 g per kilo of reference weight when the goal is to lose weight with at least three sessions a week, and 1.8 g below that. The details are in how much protein per day.

Training during a cut: keep the loads

The signal that tells the body to keep muscle is training that works it. Sharply cutting loads or volume “because you are cutting” removes that signal just when it matters most. It is normal for performance to progress less, or even dip a little at the end of a cut; a rapid drop in loads, on the other hand, is a sign the deficit is too steep.

To follow this without a spreadsheet, Basal shows your loads and your estimated 1RM per exercise, next to your weight trend. The volume each muscle group gets is counted in Premium (sets per muscle).

A cut that stalls: what to do

After a few weeks, the loss almost always slows: a lighter body burns less, and attention to the log fades. Before cutting further, check those two points, then read the guide to the weight loss plateau. A one-to-two-week break at maintenance is a studied option [4]; Basal suggests it after eight weeks of deficit if the loss has stopped.

What we don’t know

Most studies on cutting involve small groups of athletes, often men, over a few weeks. The ranges above are averages: the share of muscle lost varies from person to person, and no app measures it directly. Weight, waist size and how your loads change remain indirect indicators.
