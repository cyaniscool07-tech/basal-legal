Weighing food raw or cooked: which to choose, and the mistake to avoid

Pasta, rice or lentils double in weight when cooked, and chicken loses a fifth. Both ways of weighing are correct, on one condition: choose the food in the table in the same state as what you put on the scale. Here are the numbers, and what the mistake costs.

In short

Weigh raw when you can, and choose the “raw” or “dry” food in the table: that figure does not depend on how you cook. If you can only weigh cooked, choose the “cooked” food. The mistake to avoid is mixing the two: 80 g of dry pasta is 291 kcal, but if you weigh the 174 g of cooked pasta it makes and choose “pasta, raw”, you count 633, more than double.

Why weight changes when cooking

Cooking neither creates nor destroys calories in any meaningful amount. What changes is water. Dry starches (pasta, rice, couscous, quinoa, pulses) absorb it: their weight is multiplied by two to three, and their calories per 100 g are divided accordingly. Meat and fish lose it, with a little fat: they weigh less after cooking, and each 100 g is more calorie-dense.

The same food therefore has two values per 100 g, both correct. The ANSES Ciqual table used by Basal often gives both.

On starches, the mistake is counted in hundreds of calories per plate, either way. On meat it is smaller, around forty calories, but it also affects protein. Repeated every day, it is enough to explain why a deficit “on paper” does not move the scale (see the calorie deficit).

The simplest method

  • Weigh raw or dry, before cooking, when possible. The figure depends neither on cooking time, nor on drained water, nor on your saucepan. It is also, most often, the state shown on packets.
  • If you can only weigh cooked (canteen, ready meal, yesterday’s leftovers), choose the “cooked” entry. The result will be a little less precise, but correct.
  • Read the food’s name before confirming. In a search, “white rice, raw” and “white rice, cooked” come one after the other. That is where the mistake slips in.
  • Keep the same habit. A small constant error corrects itself if your expenditure is recalculated from your actual weight. An error that changes from day to day does not.

One dish for several servings

For a dish cooked in one go (bolognese, curry, chilli), you cannot weigh each serving raw. The reliable method:

  1. Weigh all the ingredients raw and add up their calories.
  2. Weigh the whole dish once cooked, without the pan.
  3. Calories in your serving = total × (weight of your serving ÷ weight of the cooked dish).

In Basal, a recipe is entered with raw quantities, and a batch can record the weight of the cooked dish: the app then shows the cooked weight of one serving, to weigh on your plate. The meal prep guide covers how to store these dishes.

What we do not know

Your cooking yield. Very soft pasta absorbs more water than al dente pasta, well-done meat loses more than rare. A table’s “cooked” values correspond to average cooking.

Cooking fat. A spoonful of oil is about 90 kcal, and it appears in neither the raw nor the cooked value of a table. It is counted separately.

The share of fat lost by meat. It varies with the cut and the cooking. That is why the table’s weights after cooking are an order of magnitude for meat.
