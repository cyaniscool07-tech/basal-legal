Diet break and refeed: pausing your diet without losing your progress

Eating at maintenance in the middle of a diet feels like admitting defeat. Yet it is a studied strategy, in two forms: the diet break, a one-to-two-week pause, and the refeed, a single higher day from time to time. What we know about them is encouraging, but more modest than what you often read.

In short

A diet break is one to two weeks at maintenance, then back to the deficit. A refeed is one or two days at maintenance, mostly from carbohydrates, at regular intervals. Both make a diet easier to stick to; their effect on fat loss itself is debated, and they lengthen the total duration of the diet.

Diet break or refeed: the difference

A diet break puts the deficit on hold for one to two weeks: you eat at your maintenance, across all macros, then resume the diet. A refeed is shorter, one or two days, and mainly raises carbohydrates, to refill glycogen and get a mental breather.

Both differ from a reverse diet, which ends the diet for good, and from an unplanned cheat day, which has neither a duration nor a target.

Diet break: what the research says

The most cited trial, MATADOR, compared, in men with obesity, 16 weeks of continuous dieting with a diet of the same total dieting time broken up by two-week breaks at energy balance. The group with breaks lost more fat, and kept it off better six months later [1]. It also took nearly twice as long to finish.

In strength-trained people, the ICECAP trial found no clear difference in fat loss or lean mass between continuous dieting and dieting with a one-week break every three weeks [2]. Participants with breaks did, however, report less hunger. A review on athletes concludes that the main benefit probably lies there: making the diet sustainable [3].

Refeed: what it is really for

In a trial in trained people, two high-carbohydrate refeed days a week preserved lean mass better than continuous dieting over seven weeks, without changing fat loss [4]. The effect is real but modest, and the trial involved few people.

The often-repeated idea that a refeed day durably “boosts” metabolism or hormones has not been shown: the measured effects on leptin are short-lived. The most solid gain is practical: a planned landmark that heads off the unplanned binge.

Diet break: when and for how long

The protocols studied range from one week off every three weeks to two weeks off every two weeks. In practice, a diet break makes most sense after several weeks of deficit, when loss slows or fatigue sets in (weight loss plateau).

Expect the scale to go up by a kilo or two during the break: replenished glycogen stores water. It is not fat, and it comes off again when the diet resumes (daily weight fluctuations).

What we don’t know

Trials on diet breaks are few, often small, and their results do not all point the same way. We do not know which frequency or duration works best, or whether the effect seen in men with obesity holds for women or for people who are already lean. The app’s default settings are cautious choices, not proven values.
