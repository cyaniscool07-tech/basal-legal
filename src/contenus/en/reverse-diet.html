Losing or gaining weight

Reverse diet: coming off a diet by raising calories in steps

After months of dieting, going straight back to “eating normally” is daunting, and often rightly so: appetite is high and reference points are blurred. A reverse diet raises calories gradually up to maintenance. What it allows is real; what is claimed for it is often exaggerated.

In short

A reverse diet raises calories in small weekly steps, from your diet intake up to maintenance, while keeping protein stable. It usually lasts four to eight weeks. It helps you get your bearings back and limits a sudden regain; nothing proves that it “boosts” metabolism beyond maintenance.

Reverse diet: the definition

A reverse diet is a gradual exit from a calorie deficit: instead of going from 1,600 to 2,200 kcal overnight, you go up in steps, week after week, until you reach the intake that keeps your weight stable. It is not a way to lose weight: it is a way to stop losing weight without regaining it all.

Reverse diet: what the research says (and what it does not)

The reverse diet comes from bodybuilding. Reviews on metabolic adaptation present it as a plausible approach, but one that has barely been studied in humans [1]. There is no trial showing that it raises expenditure beyond what the increase in calories and weight explains.

What is better documented is the value of returning to energy balance rather than prolonging a deficit indefinitely: periods at maintenance built into a diet led to better fat loss than continuous dieting in a controlled trial [2]. And expenditure really does fall during a diet [3], which makes your old maintenance misleading: the new one is lower.

Reverse diet: how to do it

  1. Estimate your current maintenance, at today’s weight and, if possible, from your real data (work out your real expenditure), not the one from before the diet.
  2. Work out the gap between your current intake and that maintenance.
  3. Go up one step each week, 50 to 150 kcal, adding mostly carbohydrates: they refill glycogen stores and support training.
  4. Keep protein stable (protein per day).
  5. Stop going up at maintenance, and stay there.

Expect to gain a little weight at first: glycogen stores water. That is mass, not fat (daily weight fluctuations).

Reverse diet: how long it takes

The duration follows from the gap and the step. Basal splits the gap into about six steps, rounded to 10 kcal, between 50 and 150 kcal a week. For an intake of 1,600 kcal and a maintenance of 2,000 kcal, the gap is 400 kcal: the step is 70 kcal, and the climb takes six weeks. With these limits, most climbs take four to eight weeks.

Reverse diet or diet break: which to choose

A break (diet break) puts the deficit on hold, one to two weeks at maintenance, then resumes it: it is useful when loss stalls and there is still some way to go (weight loss plateau). A reverse diet ends it: there is no automatic return to dieting. You choose it when the goal is reached, or after a very long diet.

What we don’t know

We do not know whether a reverse diet limits weight regain better than going straight back to maintenance: no controlled trial has compared them. The choice of step (50 to 150 kcal a week) is a rule of caution, not a study result. What a reverse diet reliably brings is a gradual, measured return that gives you time to get your bearings back.
