Basal metabolic rate: what it is and what changes it

Your basal metabolic rate is the energy your body spends at complete rest, to breathe, keep your heart beating and hold its temperature. In a low-activity adult, it is the largest part of the day’s expenditure. Here is how it is estimated, what really changes it, and what a formula cannot know about you.

In short

Basal metabolic rate is usually between 1,200 and 2,000 kcal a day in adults. It depends above all on your lean mass (muscles and organs), so on your weight and height, far more than on your age. A formula like Mifflin-St Jeor estimates it to within about 10% for most people, but it can be further off for you.

Definition

The basal metabolic rate (BMR) is the energy expenditure of a person who is awake, lying down, at complete rest, twelve hours after their last meal, in a room at neutral temperature. It is the running cost of the body: heart, breathing, brain, liver, kidneys, cell renewal, temperature control.

When measured in a lab, it is more often called resting metabolic rate (RMR), with slightly less strict conditions and often a slightly higher value. The usual formulas, including the one Basal uses, actually estimate this resting expenditure. The difference is small and everyday language mixes the two.

Its share of expenditure

Your daily expenditure has three blocks:

  • basal metabolic rate, about 60 to 70% of expenditure in a low-activity person;
  • digestion (the thermic effect of food), about 10%;
  • activity: sport, but above all all the movement of daily life (walking, standing, moving around). It is the block that varies most from one person to another.

That is why your basal metabolic rate is not what you should eat: it is the starting point. To get your total expenditure, it is multiplied by an activity factor, from 1.2 for a sedentary person to 1.9 for intense daily sport. The calorie calculator does both steps.

How it is estimated

Direct measurement uses indirect calorimetry: a device analyses the oxygen consumed and the carbon dioxide breathed out over twenty to thirty minutes. It is found in hospitals and some labs, rarely elsewhere.

Day to day, an equation is used. Basal uses Mifflin-St Jeor (1990), which the American Dietetic Association’s systematic review (Frankenfield, 2005) judged the most reliable of the common equations in adults, including overweight adults:

  • man: 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age + 5
  • woman: 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age − 161

The older Harris-Benedict equation (1919) overestimates the expenditure of today’s populations on average. If you know your lean mass, for example from a DEXA scan, the Katch-McArdle equation (370 + 21.6 × lean mass in kg) takes it into account directly. Bioimpedance scales give a lean mass too imprecise to benefit from it.

What changes it

A study of 150 adults whose basal metabolic rate was measured (Johnstone, 2005) looked at what explains the differences between people:

  • lean mass alone explains about 63% of the differences;
  • fat mass, about 6%: it spends little, but it does spend;
  • age, about 2%;
  • about a quarter of the differences remain unexplained.

Once lean mass is accounted for, sex adds almost nothing. Muscle counts, but less than often claimed: a kilo of muscle at rest spends around 13 kcal a day, while the liver, brain, heart and kidneys, which weigh little, spend much more. Gaining 2 kg of muscle raises basal metabolic rate by a few dozen kcal, not several hundred.

Weight loss, on the other hand, lowers it: a lighter body spends less. Part of that drop goes beyond what the weight loss explains — that is metabolic adaptation, and it is why a formula calculated once is not enough during a diet.

Age, really?

The formula takes off 5 kcal per year of age: an average observed across groups, which mixes age with the muscle loss that often comes with it. A large study of over 6,400 people (Pontzer, 2021) found that expenditure, adjusted for body size and composition, stays stable between 20 and 60, then falls by about 0.7% a year.

In other words, “metabolism slows down at 40” is mainly true when you lose muscle and move less. Staying active and strength training limit most of the decline.

Eating below your basal metabolic rate

Since total expenditure is higher than basal metabolic rate, eating a little below it does not mean the body “shuts down”. But such a low intake leaves little room for protein, fibre and micronutrients, and adds to fatigue and muscle loss.

Basal therefore applies a simple rule: the suggested calorie target never goes below your estimated basal metabolic rate. It is a cautious choice by the app, not a physiological law. The calorie deficit guide details a reasonable pace of loss.

What we do not know

Your exact number. A formula gives the average of people who resemble you on paper. For some people, the gap with a measurement exceeds 10%, that is 150 kcal or more.

Your lean mass. It explains most of the differences, but no formula based on weight and height knows it.

The effect of sleep, stress or cold on your day. It exists, but it is small and hard to put a number on for a given person.

The only way to correct the estimate without a lab is to compare what you eat with how your weight actually changes over a few weeks. That is what the real expenditure calculator does, and what the app does continuously.
