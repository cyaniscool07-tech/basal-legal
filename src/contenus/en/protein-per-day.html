How much protein per day?

To cover an adult’s needs, 0.83 g per kilo of body weight is enough. With strength training, or during weight loss, the useful range rises to 1.4 to 2.2 g per kilo. Here is where those figures come from, how to apply them to your weight, and what we do not know.

In short

Without particular sport, aim for about 0.83 g of protein per kilo a day (60 g at 72 kg). With strength training, 1.4 to 2 g per kilo. During weight loss with training, rather the top of the range, up to 2.2 g, to protect muscle. Above a BMI of 25, calculate on a reference weight rather than your total weight.

The minimum for an adult

ANSES (France) and EFSA use the same reference for a healthy adult: 0.83 g of protein per kilo of body weight per day. This figure covers the needs of 97.5% of the population. It is a floor to avoid deficiency, not a target for building muscle.

Older people are an exception: the PROT-AGE expert group recommends 1.0 to 1.2 g per kilo after 65, because muscle responds less well to protein with age.

The most cited meta-analysis on strength training (Morton, 2018, 49 trials) finds that muscle gain stops increasing at around 1.6 g per kilo on average. Its confidence interval goes up to 2.2 g, hence the advice to aim higher if you want to be sure you are not limited.

In a deficit, needs go up: the body draws more readily on muscle when energy is short. Reviews of lean athletes in calorie restriction put the useful intake higher than at maintenance, hence the top of the range during weight loss. The rest of the strategy is in the calorie deficit guide.

If your weight is high

Protein needs follow lean mass (muscles, organs), not fat. Multiplying 2.2 g by 120 kg would give 264 g a day, over 1,000 kcal of protein: no recommendation supports such a figure.

The usual correction is the adjusted weight. Up to a BMI of 25, your actual weight is kept. Above it, the weight corresponding to a BMI of 25 is used, plus a quarter of the excess. That is what Basal does, and what the protein calculator does.

reference weight = weight at BMI 25 + 0.25 × (weight − weight at BMI 25)

Spreading it over the day

The day’s total matters more than the timing. A regular spread still helps: the review by Schoenfeld and Aragon (2018) suggests about 0.4 g per kilo per meal, over at least four meals, to reach 1.6 g per kilo. At 70 kg, that is close to 30 g per meal.

In practice, a serving of protein at each meal is usually enough. The poorest meals are usually breakfast and snacks: that is where quark, eggs or skyr change the total most.

Too much protein, a risk?

In healthy adults, EFSA considers intakes of up to twice the reference safe, about 1.7 g per kilo. It has not set an upper limit, for lack of sufficient data beyond. Sports ranges up to 2.2 g go beyond that benchmark: they rest on studies in healthy people, often lasting a few months.

Kidney disease changes everything: in that case, protein intake is decided with a doctor, not a calculator.

What we do not know

Your exact need. These ranges are group averages. Your real need depends on your lean mass, your training and your age, which no formula measures.

The real gain beyond 1.6 g per kilo. It is small and uncertain. Aiming higher mostly serves as a safety margin.

The very long-term effects of high intakes. Trials rarely last more than a year.
