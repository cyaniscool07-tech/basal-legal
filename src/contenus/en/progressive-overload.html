Progressive overload: how to keep progressing in the gym

A muscle adapts to what you ask of it, then stops adapting if you always ask for the same thing. Progressive overload simply means asking for a little more, regularly and measurably. The principle fits in one sentence; applying it mostly takes knowing what you did last time.

In short

Progressive overload means gradually increasing the effort: more load, more reps or more sets. Adding reps gives results comparable to adding load. The simplest rule: once you go past the top of your rep range on every set, raise the load by about 2 to 5%.

Progressive overload: the definition

Progressive overload is the principle that training has to become slightly more demanding over time to keep producing progress. The body responds to a stress by adapting to it; once adapted, the same stress is no longer enough. It is not one method among others: it is what every strength training method has in common.

The levers of progressive overload: load, reps, sets

You can make a session more demanding in several ways:

  • load: lifting heavier for the same number of reps;
  • reps: doing more reps with the same load;
  • sets: adding a set, and so volume (sets per muscle);
  • execution: same load, fuller range of motion or better-controlled tempo.

A 2022 study compared two groups over eight weeks: one increased the load, the other the reps. Muscle gains were comparable, with a slight strength advantage for the group that raised the load [1]. Both routes work.

Progressive overload: how fast to increase

The American College of Sports Medicine recommends increasing the load by about 2 to 10% once you can do one or two reps more than planned in two consecutive sessions [2]. In practice, 2.5 kg is often already a lot on upper-body lifts; a beginner progresses almost every session, someone with several years of training over weeks.

A simple method is double progression: pick a range, for example 8 to 12 reps. Increase the reps session after session; when every set reaches 12, add load and work back up from 8.

Heavy or light loads: what really matters

For muscle size, a meta-analysis found no clear difference between heavy sets and lighter sets, as long as they are taken close to failure; for maximal strength, heavy loads keep the edge [3]. What matters is that the effort increases, not how it increases.

Weekly volume also plays a part: more sets per muscle give more growth on average, up to a point [4].

When progress stops

Sooner or later, the load stops going up. Before changing programme, check three things: sleep, calories and protein. In a calorie deficit, progressing gets harder, and holding your loads is already a good result (cutting). If everything is in place, switching lever often helps: moving from load to reps, or adding a set.

Progressive overload in Basal: knowing what you did last time

During a session, each exercise shows your last performance, with its date and sets, for example “60 kg × 10, 9, 8”. That is the information you need in the gym to decide whether to raise the load or the reps. Warm-up sets are not counted, so a light set before the real weight does not skew anything.

Each exercise page tracks top weight, estimated 1RM (Epley formula: 80 kg × 8 reps gives about 101 kg), volume and reps, and marks your records. Volume is deliberately left out of records: making it a record would push you to lengthen sessions rather than progress. The volume each muscle group gets is counted in Premium.

What we don’t know

There is no ideal rate of progress that holds for everyone: it depends on training age, the exercise, recovery and diet. Studies compare groups over a few weeks, rarely over several years. The estimated 1RM is an estimate: the formula becomes less reliable beyond about ten reps.
