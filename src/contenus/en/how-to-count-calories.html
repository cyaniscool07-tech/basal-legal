How to count calories: the simple method, and the mistakes that skew it

Counting calories has a bad reputation: tedious, obsessive, imprecise. That is true when it is done badly. Done well, it takes a few minutes a day, a few weeks of learning, and leaves you with reference points that stay even when you stop logging.

In short

Weigh what you can for the first few weeks, log at mealtime rather than in the evening, and pick the food in the right state (raw or cooked). The most common omissions are oil, sauces, drinks and snacks. The time it takes drops quickly: in one study, it went from about 23 to 15 minutes a day over six months.

Counting calories: why it works

Writing down what you eat is one of the behaviours most consistently linked to weight loss in studies [1]. It is not magic: people eat differently when they see what they eat. And without numbers, estimating by eye is very imprecise: people who could not lose weight underestimated their intake by 47% on average [2].

Counting only makes sense against a target: first you need to know how many calories a day suit you.

How to count calories: the method in 4 steps

  1. Weigh the main foods for the first few weeks, with a kitchen scale. After a few weeks, you will be able to estimate a portion of pasta or rice by eye.
  2. Pick the right food, in the right state: 100 g of raw rice and 100 g of cooked rice have very different values (weighing food raw or cooked).
  3. Log at mealtime, not in the evening from memory: that is where omissions happen.
  4. Look at the week rather than the day: one day 300 kcal over changes nothing if the average is right.

The mistakes that skew the count

It is almost never the big foods that skew the total, but what you do not think of logging:

  • cooking oil and butter: one tablespoon of oil provides about 90 kcal;
  • sauces and dressings;
  • drinks: juice, soft drinks, alcohol, sweetened coffees;
  • snacks: a handful of nuts, the children’s leftovers, tasting while cooking;
  • meals out, which are harder to estimate (fast food calories).

How long it takes

In a study of an online weight-loss programme, the time spent logging meals went from about 23 minutes a day in the first month to under 15 minutes by the sixth, and those who logged most often lost the most weight [3]. Meals repeat: once your usual breakfasts and dishes are saved, most entries take a few seconds.

Counting calories: for whom, and for how long

Counting is not a lifelong obligation. Many people use it for a few months to learn the orders of magnitude, then stop and pick it up again from time to time, for example when their weight creeps back up. If logging your meals becomes a source of anxiety or guilt, or if you have a history of eating disorders, talk to a health professional before you start.

What we don’t know

Even done well, a food log remains approximate: table values are averages, labels have a tolerance margin, and a weighed portion is never exactly the one in the table. Nor can we say how many errors it takes for a log to stop being useful. That is why Basal corrects your expenditure with your real weight rather than relying on the logged total alone.
